Bands

RGB simulation is not true colourEvery SPHEREx band lies in the infrared, beyond 0.75 µm, where the eye sees nothing. Three bands are shown as red, green and blue, so that a difference between bands is seen as a colour.

Which band is which colourR = D6 4.42–5.00 µm, else D5 3.83–4.41
G = D4 2.42–3.82 µm, else D3 1.63–2.42
B = D2 1.10–1.62 µm, else D1 0.75–1.09

How they are chosenThe order of the wavelengths is kept, as in the eye: the longest in red, the shortest in blue. The six bands form three pairs, short, middle and long, one pair for each colour, so the three colours cover the whole range. In a pair the longer band is used; where it did not see the place, the other stands in.

White balanceEach band is set on one scale, from its sky to its bright stars, so the brightest stars come out near white. Redder than them means relatively brighter at the long wavelengths, as cool or dust-covered objects are; bluer, at the short ones.

Not one momentSPHEREx sees a place with one or two bands on a day. A colour missing on a date is taken from the nearest date that has it, and is marked with * on the picture and "(from date)" under "This frame".

Preliminary reading

Written by fixed rules from the points of this curve: where it peaks, how steeply it rises or falls, the blackbody closest to it, and whether known bands and lines are there. The wavelengths looked for are reference values: atomic lines from the NIST Atomic Spectra Database, bands of molecules, ices and dust from the papers listed under the reading. For a galaxy or quasar with a published redshift, the lines are looked for where that redshift puts them.

No language model is involved, and the object is not looked up to decide what the curve shows.

        Distance and redshift

        These values are looked up, not measured from the SPHEREx images, except the row “From its lines”. The object at the crosshair is identified in SIMBAD (CDS, Strasbourg).

        A star gets its distance from its parallax: ESA Gaia DR3, with the distance Bailer-Jones et al. (2021) derive from it, or the parallax SIMBAD holds for stars too bright for Gaia.

        A galaxy or a quasar gets its published redshift (SIMBAD, then NED). How long its light travelled and how far it is now follow from the redshift in the Planck 2018 cosmology.

        “From its lines” is our own estimate. Where the spectrum shows emission lines, the mean quasar spectrum is laid on it at every redshift, and a value is given only when at least two lines stand on their places, Hα among them. Spectra without clear lines, and stars, get none.

          Measured in this field

          The object is found on the detector pixels of every SPHEREx exposure and compared with where the Gaia catalogue says it should be on that date. A straight-line motion is then fitted through the positions.

            About the data

            Every picture, spectrum and plot of flux over time on this site is made from public SPHEREx Level 2 spectral images, at the moment a view is opened. This page says what those files are and what is done to them.

            The files

            SPHEREx looks at the sky through linear variable filters: the wavelength changes across each detector. One exposure shows a star at one wavelength, and many exposures of the same star add up to its spectrum. A position is seen again about every six months, many times during each visit.

            A Level 2 file is one calibrated exposure of one detector: 2040 × 2040 pixels of 6.15″, about 3.5° on a side, in a FITS file of 62 to 65 MB with these extensions.

            IMAGESurface brightness in MJy/sr, 32-bit float, with the astrometry (TAN projection with SIP distortion)
            FLAGSOne bit per pixel for each condition: hot, saturated, hit by a cosmic ray, on a known source and so on
            VARIANCEVariance of the image, in (MJy/sr)²
            ZODIModel of the zodiacal light in the image
            EPSFPoint spread function in each zone of the detector
            WCS-WAVECentral wavelength and bandwidth across the detector

            The six bands

            Six detectors cover 0.75 to 5.0 µm in 102 spectral channels.

            From file to picture

            1. Find. The archive is asked which exposures cover the position, in the releases DR1 and QR3. Where both hold an exposure, the newer processing is used.
            2. Cut out. Only the rows around the position are read from each file, about 1.5 MB instead of the whole file.
            3. Mask. Pixels flagged as transient, saturated, hot, cold, non-linear, persistent, outlier, snowball, missing or not working are left out. Pixels on a known source are kept, and the saturated cores of bright stars are painted bright instead of left as holes.
            4. Subtract the background. A plane (a level and a slope) is fitted to the sky of each cutout with 2.5σ clipping and taken away. This removes the zodiacal light, which is far brighter than most sources and different in every exposure.
            5. Align. Each cutout is resampled through its own astrometry onto one grid: north up, east left, centred on the position, with 3.075″ pixels (6.15″ for fields wider than 12′).
            6. Group. Exposures are shown one by one, or combined by day or by sky pass. Each is divided by its own noise first, so bands of very different brightness can share a frame.
            7. Show. One asinh stretch is used for the whole sequence. In the RGB simulation (not true colour: every band is infrared), red is D6, green is D4 and blue is D2.

            Measurements

            The spectrum and the plot over time are aperture photometry on each exposure, before any resampling: a radius of 2 pixels, the sky taken from a ring of 5 to 10 pixels, in microjansky. The wavelength of each point is the one the filter gives at that spot of the detector.

            Stars that saturate are measured from the halo around the core, with a systematic error of 20 to 25%. These are quick-look values, not an official SPHEREx data product. For publication, measure on the original files.

            The preliminary reading under the spectrum is written by fixed rules from those points: where the curve peaks, its slope, the blackbody closest to it, and bands and lines at reference wavelengths (water vapour at 1.4, 1.9 and 2.7 µm, methane at 3.3 µm, water ice at 3.0 µm, carbon dioxide at 4.27 µm, lines of hydrogen, helium, sulphur and iron, the 3.3 µm PAH band). The wavelengths of atomic lines are the vacuum values of the NIST Atomic Spectra Database (version 5.12); the bands of water vapour and methane are the band origins of Polyansky et al. (2018) and of Shimanouchi's tables (NIST Chemistry WebBook), those of ices come from Boogert et al. (2015) and the PAH band from van Diedenhoven et al. (2004). Every row of the list under the reading names its source, and each number was read in that source. For an object with a published redshift the lines are looked for where the redshift puts them, which brings the lines of visible and ultraviolet light into range. A band or line counts when it stands at least 4σ from the continuum beside it. The reading describes the curve and lists the kinds of object that fit; it is not a classification, and it says how far it can be trusted.

            Moving objects

            When a wide field has loaded, its exposures are searched for anything that moves, with no catalogue to guide the search: what appears in single exposures is linked into straight paths, each path is checked in the pixels of every exposure along it, and only then compared with the known asteroids and comets.

            How much such a search finds cannot be read off its results, so it was measured: fake asteroids of known brightness and speed were added to the real exposures of three fields and searched for with the same code. The share found, by brightness, speed and field, is at the end of the page.

            Get the originals

            The Source files panel (Advanced) lists the exposures behind a view. Each one links to its file at NASA/IPAC IRSA, and the download script and URL list fetch them from the archive straight to your computer.
